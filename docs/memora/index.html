The problem

Memory has to change, not just be stored

Most memory benchmarks test recall: can the agent find something it was told a session or two ago? For memory to stay useful over long time horizons, it has to be updated as things change. Memora’s contribution is a dataset built with more updates and deletions before each question, and more earlier sessions that have to be combined to answer it.

Task-dependent

All six memory systems beat every LLM on remembering. Recommending is close, and reasoning is low for all systems (30 at best).

Longer timelines score lower

Most systems drop from weekly to quarterly. MemoBase falls from 44 to 15 on remembering.

The forgetting penalty

Scoring only what’s mentioned overstates performance. The penalty for outdated items affects memory systems more as timelines get longer.

Using the same answers and judge model, and changing only the judge prompt, every one of the 82 verdicts that flipped went from “forgot” to “remembered”. The paper does not specify which interpretation is intended.

ComplicatesAbout a third of a typical recommending score requires no memory at all, and how the judge interprets the word “mention” moves the forgetting score by more than 30 points.

D · Gaps in their pipeline

What their pipeline doesn’t check

The paperThe paper scores against the simulator’s plan, and never checks the conversations against it, or the neutral sessions at all.

From my projectPlanned change vs what the user said
planned (session 36)update liked artist: Queen → Guns N’ Roses
→
said“I used to be all about Queen, but now I’m really into Guns N’ Roses!”

On my development personas nearly every planned change is said in the conversation, so scoring against the plan mostly works. The paper never checks this. Activity records are another matter: the plan holds the whole record, the user says only what changed, and only about a third of planned values appear word for word.

From my projectA fact from a neutral session
“…you find reading relaxing and are interested in ancient philosophies and how their ideas resonate today…”

Found while hand-labelling: this came from a neutral small-talk session. It’s true, relevant and helpful, and the rubric can’t see it. Their quality loop skips those sessions entirely.

Mostly confirmsThe plan and the conversations mostly agree. Facts leaking in from unchecked neutral sessions seem rare in what I read, but I don’t have a count.

If you build memory agents
  1. Test memory on the model you’ll actually use. Their memory systems all ran on GPT-4o-mini, so their scores say little about your model. On Qwen3.6-35B, memory added 40 to 50 points.
  2. Check how a benchmark’s judge reads your answers. Changing only the judge prompt moved my forgetting score by more than 30 points.
  3. Give the answer step a sense of time. Show it when each memory was saved and what “today” is; hiding the dates cost about 12 points.
What my results can’t tell you
  • Weekly timeline only. Memora also has monthly and quarterly timelines, with about 4 and 13 times as many sessions.
  • 45 questions. Only the 3 development personas; the other 7 stay held out for my project, so the ranges are wide.
  • My models, not theirs. I used Qwen3.6-35B to answer and Gemma to judge, not GPT-4o-mini and their three-judge vote, so compare the differences within my runs, not my scores with their table.
  • My judge is an LLM too. Checked against 70 of my hand labels, not a full human study.
Open questionWhen is it appropriate to remember what has been forgotten? How does this change based on our expectations for computational systems?
