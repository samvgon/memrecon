82 of 296 forgetting verdicts flip, every one toward “mentioned”. The judges don’t favour their own model family’s answers (E44).

Each “task” is really one kind of memory (all 150 weekly questions, E52)
Recommending=preferences 50forgetting checks: 50 of 50
Reasoning=expense & step logs 30goals 20forgetting checks: 0 of 50
Remembering=work documents 38to-dos, calendar 12forgetting checks: 48 of 50

So “reasoning is hardest” could just as well mean “summing a long log is hardest”. Reasoning is 8 question texts asked 50 times (“What is my total food spending this week?”), and an outdated number only shows up as a wrong total.

ComplicatesA third of a typical recommending score needs no memory, and the forgetting penalty depends on how a judge reads one word.

D · Was the data checked?

Checks I ran before trusting the data

Planned change vs what the user said
planned (session 36)update liked artist: Queen → Guns N’ Roses
→
said“I used to be all about Queen, but now I’m really into Guns N’ Roses!”

On my development personas nearly every planned change is said in the conversation (E26), so scoring against the plan mostly works. The paper never checks this. Activity records are another matter: the plan holds the whole record, the user says only what changed, and only about a third of planned values appear word for word (E06).

A fact from a session nobody checked
“…you find reading relaxing and are interested in ancient philosophies and how their ideas resonate today…”

Found while hand-labelling: this came from a neutral small-talk session. It’s true, relevant and helpful, and the rubric can’t see it. Their quality loop skips those sessions entirely.

ConfirmsThe plan and the conversation mostly agree, so their scores hold up here. They just never showed it.
